# The top 10% of UK general-practice users accounted for about four in ten consultations

## Finding

Across 845 UK general practices from 2000–2001 to 2018–2019, patients above each practice-year's 90th centile of consultation count accounted for approximately four in ten consultations. Their median share of face-to-face GP consultations increased from 38.0% to 43.0%, while their share of all GP consultations increased from 38.0% to 40.9%. Frequent attenders consulted about five times as often as the remainder of the practice population.

## Source context

`SRC-0032` analysed 1.70 billion CPRD GOLD consultation events from 12.33 million patients. Consultations included repeat-prescription requests and contacts with practice staff; routine reception contacts were generally administrative episodes and not counted as consultations.

## Population and geography

Registered patients in 845 UK general practices contributing CPRD GOLD data. The principal estimates combine England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

## Method

Retrospective cohort analysis aggregated by practice and financial year. Frequent attenders were defined within each year as patients above the 90th centile of consultation count. Sensitivity analyses used the median, 75th centile and at least 12 annual consultations.

## Time period

2000-04-01 to 2019-03-31.

## Applicability to Scotland

Transferability is **medium for the concentration mechanism** and **low for exact percentages**. Scotland shares the UK primary-care context and Scottish practices were represented, particularly in later years, but the main estimates are pooled and CPRD geography was affected by clustered Vision-system use.

## Limitations

The top 10% is a relative utilisation definition, not a stable clinical group. Practice participation varied over time and only 113 practices contributed throughout. Counts do not reveal contact reason, need, complexity, avoidability or suitability for another channel. UK-wide results cannot be silently converted into Scottish estimates.
